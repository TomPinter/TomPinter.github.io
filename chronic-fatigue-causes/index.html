Chronic Fatigue: The Causes Your Blood Test Was Never Designed to Find

By Tom Pinter  ·  September 24, 2026  ·  10 min read

You're tired. Not one bad night tired — months, maybe years tired. Coffee in the morning, a wall at 3 p.m., and by evening you're running on fumes despite going to bed at a reasonable hour.

So you get bloodwork done. Everything comes back normal. Your doctor shrugs: "You're healthy. It's probably just stress. Get some sleep."

But you don't feel healthy. And you do sleep.

Here's the thing nobody explains: standard blood tests are designed to detect disease — not to measure the gap between sick and optimal. "Your labs are normal" means you are not detectably diseased by current measures. That is not the same as energized. Fatigue lives almost entirely in that gap, and the causes behind it are rarely on any panel.

I've been illness-free for 50 years, and in that time — through my own experimentation and thousands of conversations — these are the causes of chronic tiredness I keep finding. None of them show up in a blood test. All of them are fixable.

(This is the fatigue-specific view of a framework I map in full in Your Doctor Says Nothing Is Wrong — 7 Hidden Causes of Chronic Symptoms. If you want the complete map first, start there.)

Cause 1 — Your cells are short on building materials, not energy

When people feel tired, the instinct is to reach for stimulants. But fatigue is often not an energy shortage — it's a materials shortage.

The body is constantly repairing and rebuilding cells. Those building materials are protein, fat and minerals. (Fat is both for energy and needed to build cell membranes, hormones etc.) Many people eat foods high in carbohydrates — such as pasta, bread, and potatoes — which provide energy but very little raw material for repair.

The consequence: the repair work slows, and the body reports that as being run down. You can sleep ten hours and still feel flat, because the problem was never in the battery — it was in the supply line.

I know this one personally. When I ate average-quality foods and skipped supplements for just a few days — normal home-cooked meals, not junk — I developed clear deficiency symptoms. Noticeable enough to be unambiguous. And when I've recommended full-spectrum micronutrients to people who felt nothing from single supplements, the common report after a few weeks is the same: a lift in everyday energy that they didn't realize they were missing.

What matters is the complete range — the roughly 90 essential nutrients (60 minerals, 16 vitamins, 12 essential amino acids, 3 essential fatty acids) — not megadoses of one thing. I've written out that framework in full in I've Taken Supplements Every Day for 50 Years.

Cause 2 — The insulin roller-coaster

Here is a cause of fatigue that almost nobody talks about outside nutrition circles, and that I haven't published before.

It's not just how many carbohydrates you eat — it's how your body reacts to them. Carbohydrates that trigger a violent insulin response can, over time, exhaust the hormonal system. And an exhausted hormonal system feels exactly like what you're feeling at 3 p.m.: drained, foggy, craving fuel again an hour after eating.

This is the same logic behind why a sugar-heavy diet made me gain weight when I was young — the unused energy turns into fat — but the fatigue angle is the one people miss. If your daily eating pattern whips your insulin up and down several times a day, you are asking a hormonal system to run a marathon. Eventually something has to slow down. It slows down everywhere at once, and you call it tiredness.

I'm describing what I have observed in myself and in decades of practice, not prescribing a diet. But if your bloodwork is normal and your energy crashes anyway, what you eat and how sharply it moves your insulin is the first place I would look.

Cause 3 — Thirst disguised as hunger

Mild chronic dehydration doesn't announce itself with a dry mouth. It announces itself as tiredness — and, misleadingly, as hunger.

The body often disguises thirst as hunger. Many people neglect drinking enough water, so the body tries to get it indirectly through food — since food also contains some water, which is better than nothing. A similar thing happens when someone craves sweets, but after drinking a big glass of water, the craving disappears.

It's worth trying!

Two habits I've kept for decades tell me instantly where I stand: I always keep a large bottle of water nearby and pay attention to the color of my urine. And many times, when I feel hunger two or three hours after a proper meal, I drink a big glass of water and the feeling goes away.

There's a mineral side to this too. Water balance is regulated through salts — so when you sweat out salt without replacing it, plain water alone isn't enough. A lack of salt can lead to headaches or even fainting in hot weather. During summer heat, sauna sessions, intense exercise or physical labor, you either need saltier foods or a little salt added directly to your drinking water. The generic "drink X glasses a day" advice misses the point entirely: your need depends on your climate, your sweat, and your food. Learn to listen to what your body needs.

Cause 4 — A drainage system with no pump

Fatigue isn't only about what's missing. Sometimes it's about what's not moving.

Your circulatory system has a pump for blood — the heart. But the lymphatic system, which clears cellular waste and supports immune function, has no pump at all. While the heart pumps blood, it's muscle activity that moves the lymph.

Sit all day, and your clearance system stagnates. Stagnation feels heavy, sluggish, puffy — tired.

I learned the movement side of this the slow way. As a child I joined a cycling club to lose weight; months in, nothing changed. I switched to strength training, and as my muscles grew, the fat began to disappear. Looking back, it's logical — more muscle burns more energy, and more muscle also means a bigger pump for the lymph. Daily movement of any kind — even a walk outside — keeps both systems working. The tiredness of a sedentary week is not a sleep problem. It's a drainage problem.

Cause 5 — Sleep habits that undo the sleep

You can spend eight hours in bed and wake up unrefreshed, because hours are not the same as quality.

During sleep, the body performs an incredible amount of repair and regeneration. When it gets dark, we should start feeling sleepy on our own — the brain produces melatonin, a hormone that induces sleep. We shouldn't interfere with that process — for example, by staring at bright screens late at night. A blue-light filter can help. It's best to get a few hours of sleep before midnight, and wake up naturally, feeling rested. Night shifts and irregular schedules are not ideal in this respect.

And then there's the mind. Some people, no matter what they do, still struggle with sleep. In my experience, stressful thoughts are usually to blame. The mind needs to be calmed so it doesn't keep spinning on problems through the night — meditation, journaling, or physical activity can help with that. Meditation has been part of my own daily routine for so long that the line between rest and practice blurred: since a breakthrough in my practice about six months ago, I've needed noticeably less sleep, not more.

One more factor worth checking: where you sleep. In modern culture, people sleep in the same spot for years or even decades. Underground water veins and geopathic stress under the bed can be harmful, and electrosmog from bedroom wiring is a real consideration — at minimum, cut power to unnecessary devices at night. If you've slept badly in the same location for years, the environment itself may be the cause — not your habits.

Cause 6 — No goal worth getting up for

This is the cause I hesitate to write, because it's the one medicine never asks about — and in my experience, one of the most common.

People feel tired when there is nothing exciting they are working toward in their life — when they are simply grinding through work they stopped caring about, listlessly, day after day.

That kind of tiredness doesn't respond to vitamins. It doesn't respond to sleep. Because it isn't a hardware failure — it's a signal. The body was never designed to pour energy into a life it sees no reason to engage with. In nature, stress ends when the threat passes; modern life can stretch a low-grade, meaningless tension out for decades. Being in an unpleasant situation for years — at work or at home — keeps stress hormones elevated in the background, and that bill arrives as exhaustion.

I've seen it the other way too: when someone finds a goal that genuinely pulls them forward, the fatigue often lifts without any change in diet or routine. Energy follows engagement. If your labs are normal, your nutrition is solid, your sleep is fine — and you're still drained, the honest question is not "what supplement am I missing?" It's "what am I actively working toward that excites me?"

The real solution, as I've put it before, is to build a life where there's little left to worry about — and somewhere in that build, a goal worth your mornings.

How they interact — why fixing one thing rarely fixes tiredness

Notice the pattern: none of these six is dramatic. Deficient materials, insulin whiplash, quiet dehydration, stagnant lymph, thin sleep, an aimless stretch of months — individually, each one costs you 10 percent. Together, they cost you 80 percent, and no single intervention looks like it "works."

That's why the "one magic pill" search fails, and why I don't recommend chasing any single cause in isolation. The fix is systematic: foundation first — materials, water, movement, sleep — then the hormonal piece through what and how you eat, then the honest look at where your life is going. Each layer makes the others cheaper.
